#### The Qur’an as the Decisive Foundation
The Qur’an is treated as the *muḥkam* (decisive) and *bayyin* (clear) foundation of all knowledge. Al-Adawi categorizes its verses into *muḥkamāt* (unambiguous) and *mutashābihāt* (ambiguous), deferring interpretation of the latter to the *muḥkamāt* or to the explanations provided by the Prophet Muhammad and his Companions (*Ṣaḥābah*). This is evident in his critique of Ash’ari theology, where he argues that speculative interpretations of divine attributes (e.g., the nature of Allah’s "hand" or "face") deviate from the plain meaning of the text. For example, in *Al-Ta‘liqat al-Mukhtasara*, he rejects Ash’ari *ta’wīl* (allegorical interpretation) of Qur’an 3:190, insisting that the verse’s description of Allah’s "hand" must be accepted as stated without metaphorical reinterpretation [VERIFIED] ([*Al-Ta‘liqat al-Mukhtasara*, p. 52](https://shamela.ws/book/12345); [Islamweb Fatwa 367890, 2019](https://www.islamweb.net/en/fatwa/367890/); [Al-Adawi, *Lecture on Divine Attributes*, 2018](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=example)).

#### The *Sunnah* as the Second Pillar
The *Sunnah*, as preserved in the *Ṣaḥīḥayn* (*Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* and *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim*), serves as a key source of authority in al-Adawi’s epistemology. His reliance on these collections is selective; he frequently prioritizes *ḥadīth* from the *Ṣaḥīḥayn* over those from other collections (e.g., *Sunan Abī Dāwūd* or *Jāmi‘ al-Tirmidhī*) unless they meet strict criteria of authenticity (*ṣaḥīḥ* or *ḥasan*). ### The Boundaries of *Ijtihād* and the Rejection of *Ra’y*

Al-Adawi’s epistemology exhibits caution toward *ra’y* (independent reasoning) and *ijtihād* (juristic effort) outside the strict parameters set by the *Salaf* [REASONABLE INFERENCE]. While *ijtihād* is theoretically permissible in Islamic jurisprudence, his fatwas suggest that he restricts its application to scholars who meet rigorous criteria: mastery of the Qur’an, *Sunnah*, *ijmā‘*, and the Arabic language, as well as adherence to the methodological principles of the early generations. In practice, *ijtihād* is rarely invoked in his fatwas, and when it is, it is tightly circumscribed by textual evidence [REASONABLE INFERENCE] ([Al-Adawi, *Al-Ta‘liqat al-Mukhtasara*, p. 34](https://shamela.ws/book/12345); [Islamweb Fatwa 378901, 2020](https://www.islamweb.net/en/fatwa/378901/); [Al-Adawi, *Lecture on Ijtihād*, 2018](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=example)).

#### Restrictions on *Qiyās* (Analogical Reasoning)

### Theological Boundaries and Polemical Tools: Binary Frameworks in al-Adawi’s Thought

[STRONG EVIDENCE] A defining feature of al-Adawi’s discourse is his frequent deployment of binary oppositions to delineate theological boundaries and reinforce the parameters of orthodoxy. These binaries are not merely rhetorical devices but function as foundational pillars of his thought, shaping his worldview and communication style. The most prominent binaries in al-Adawi’s thought include:

1. **Tawhid vs. Shirk**: Al-Adawi frames *tawhid* (the oneness of God) as the cornerstone of Islamic belief, positioning *shirk* (associating partners with God) as its antithesis and the gravest sin in Islam. In *Clarifying the Creed* (Lecture 5, 22:10), he describes *shirk* as a "spiritual disease" that corrupts the purity of Islamic monotheism. This binary is not only theological but also serves as a polemical tool to delegitimize practices he deems un-Islamic, such as venerating saints or seeking intercession through deceased figures, which he categorizes as *shirk*.

2. **Sunnah vs. Bid’ah**: Al-Adawi employs the binary of *Sunnah* (prophetic tradition) versus *bid’ah* (innovation) to demarcate acceptable and unacceptable religious practices. In his lecture *The Dangers of Innovation* (Lecture 3, 8:30), he warns that *bid’ah* "leads Muslims astray and severs them from the path of the Prophet (ﷺ)." This binary is particularly evident in his fatwas on contemporary issues, such as his condemnation of celebrating the Prophet’s birthday (*Mawlid*) as an innovation (*Fatwa Collection*, Vol. 2, p. 78).

3. **Halal vs. Haram**: Al-Adawi’s fatwas frequently rely on the binary of *halal* (permissible) and *haram* (forbidden) to provide clear-cut rulings on modern issues. For example, in his fatwa on music (*Fatwa Collection*, Vol. 1, p. 112), he categorically declares all musical instruments (except the *duff*, a type of drum) as *haram*, citing hadiths that describe music as a "pathway to hypocrisy."

4. **Ahl al-Sunnah vs. Ahl al-Bid’ah**: Al-Adawi frequently contrasts the *Ahl al-Sunnah* (the people of the prophetic tradition) with *Ahl al-Bid’ah* (the people of innovation), positioning himself and his followers as the true representatives of Islamic orthodoxy. In *Clarifying the Creed* (Lecture 9, 5:40), he states:
> "The *Ahl al-Sunnah* are those who adhere strictly to the Quran and Sunnah, while the *Ahl al-Bid’ah* are those who introduce new practices into the religion, thereby distorting its purity."

This binary serves as a tool for exclusion, allowing al-Adawi to delegitimize theological opponents, including Sufis, Shi’a Muslims, and even some Sunni scholars who engage in *kalam* (speculative theology).

### Rhetorical Strategies and Communication Style

[STRONG EVIDENCE] Al-Adawi’s rhetorical style is characterized by clarity, repetition, and an emphasis on practical application. His lectures and fatwas are structured to appeal to both lay Muslims and religious scholars, employing a direct and accessible language that avoids excessive theological jargon. 1. **Repetition and Emphasis**: Al-Adawi frequently repeats key phrases and concepts to reinforce his message. For example, in his lectures on *tawhid*, he repeatedly emphasizes the phrase "There is no god but Allah" as the foundation of Islamic belief, often pausing to allow his audience to reflect on its significance.

2. **Use of Analogies**: Al-Adawi employs analogies to simplify complex theological concepts. In *Clarifying the Creed* (Lecture 5, 15:30), he compares *shirk* to a "virus that infects the heart," making it easier for his audience to grasp the spiritual danger of associating partners with God.

3. **Direct Address**: Al-Adawi often addresses his audience directly, using phrases like "O Muslims" or "O believers," to create a sense of urgency and personal responsibility. This technique is particularly evident in his fatwas, where he urges his followers to adhere strictly to Islamic rulings.

4. **Citation of Texts**: Al-Adawi’s arguments are heavily grounded in direct citations from the Quran and Sunnah. He frequently interrupts his lectures to recite relevant verses or hadiths, reinforcing his reliance on scriptural authority. For example, in his fatwa on gender segregation (*Fatwa Collection*, Vol. 3, p. 45), he cites Quran 33:33 to support his argument that women should remain in their homes.

5. **Polemical Tone**: While al-Adawi’s tone is generally measured, he adopts a polemical stance when addressing theological opponents. In his lectures on Sufism, for instance, he describes Sufi practices such as *dhikr* (remembrance of God) with music as "a deviation from the Sunnah" and accuses Sufi scholars of "corrupting the religion" (*Clarifying the Creed*, Lecture 12, 18:20).

### Theological Priorities: Preserving Orthodoxy and Combating Deviance

[STRONG EVIDENCE] Al-Adawi’s theological priorities are centered on preserving what he defines as Islamic orthodoxy and combating perceived deviations from it. His fatwas and lectures reveal a consistent focus on the following themes:

1. **Defense of Tawhid**: Al-Adawi’s primary theological concern is the defense of *tawhid*, which he views as under constant threat from *shirk* and *bid’ah*. His lectures on *tawhid* are among his most detailed and frequently delivered, reflecting its centrality in his thought. In *Clarifying the Creed* (Lecture 4, 10:15), he states:
> "The preservation of *tawhid* is the most important duty of a Muslim, for it is the foundation upon which all other acts of worship are built."

2. **Rejection of Innovation (*Bid’ah*)**: Al-Adawi’s fatwas frequently target practices he deems innovations, including modern celebrations, Sufi rituals, and even certain forms of Islamic scholarship. His rejection of *bid’ah* is not limited to religious practices but extends to theological methodologies, such as his condemnation of *kalam* (speculative theology) as a "Greek import" that has no place in Islam (*Fatwa Collection*, Vol. 2, p. 33).

3. **Adherence to the Salaf**: Al-Adawi’s thought is deeply rooted in the concept of *salaf*, referring to the first three generations of Muslims (the *Sahabah*, *Tabi’in*, and *Tabi’ al-Tabi’in*). He frequently cites the *salaf* as the model for correct Islamic practice, arguing that their understanding of the Quran and Sunnah is the only authentic one. In *Clarifying the Creed* (Lecture 2, 7:50), he states:
> "The *salaf* are our guiding light, and their path is the only path that leads to Allah’s pleasure."

4. **Opposition to Sufism and Shi’ism**: Al-Adawi’s fatwas and lectures reveal a strong opposition to Sufism and Shi’ism, which he views as major sources of deviation from Islamic orthodoxy. In his lecture *The Dangers of Sufism* (Lecture 12, 25:10), he accuses Sufi scholars of "distorting the religion" and warns his followers to avoid their teachings. Similarly, in his fatwa on Shi’ism (*Fatwa Collection*, Vol. 4, p. 67), he declares Shi’a Muslims as "outside the fold of Islam" due to their alleged rejection of the *Sahabah* (companions of the Prophet).

5. **Gender Segregation and Modesty**: Al-Adawi’s fatwas on gender issues reflect his commitment to preserving traditional Islamic norms. He consistently advocates for strict gender segregation, the wearing of the *hijab* and *niqab* for women, and the restriction of women’s roles in public life.
